René Descartes

1596–1650

René Descartes was a French philosopher, mathematician, and natural philosopher. He used systematic doubt to investigate the foundations of knowledge, defended a distinction between mind and body, and also examined their union and the passions. His work connected metaphysics with the study of nature and practical life, helping to shape the questions of early modern philosophy.

Method and doubt · Mind and body · Natural philosophy

Selected works

  • Discourse on the Method (1637)
  • Meditations on First Philosophy (1641)
  • Principles of Philosophy (1644)

Related research and publications

Baruch Spinoza

1632–1677

Baruch Spinoza was born into Amsterdam’s Portuguese-Jewish community. He understood human beings as part of nature and explained reality through a single substance and its modes. His philosophy connects knowledge, emotion, freedom, and the pursuit of a flourishing life. His examination of religion and political authority also defended the freedom to think and philosophise.

Nature and substance · Emotion and freedom · Thought and toleration

Selected works

  • Theological-Political Treatise (1670)
  • Ethics (1677)
  • Political Treatise (1677)

Related research and publications

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

1646–1716

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz was a German philosopher and mathematician whose interests also included law, history, and natural science. He investigated the order of the world through sufficient reason, monads, and pre-established harmony, while considering possibility, freedom, and evil. His philosophy developed across books, short essays, and extensive correspondence rather than a single definitive systematic work.

Sufficient reason · Monads and harmony · Possibility and freedom

Selected works

  • Discourse on Metaphysics (1686)Composed in 1686; not the date of first publication.

  • Monadology (1714)Composed in 1714; not the date of first publication.

Immanuel Kant

1724–1804

Immanuel Kant was a German philosopher who lived and taught in Königsberg. His critical philosophy examined the conditions of experience and the limits of human knowledge. He placed autonomy at the centre of moral life and explored how judgments of beauty and natural purposes connect nature with freedom. His three Critiques remain central to epistemology, ethics, and aesthetics.

Critique of reason · Freedom and autonomy · Aesthetic judgment

Selected works

  • Critique of Pure Reason (1781 / 1787)
  • Critique of Practical Reason (1788)
  • Critique of the Power of Judgment (1790)
Profile and works: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy

John Locke

1632–1704

John Locke was an English philosopher who investigated the sources and limits of human understanding. He traced ideas to sensation and reflection and distinguished knowledge from opinion. His political writings examined natural rights, consent, and the limits of government, while his account of religious toleration addressed the relationship between conscience, civil authority, and peaceful coexistence.

Experience and knowledge · Rights and consent · Religious toleration

Selected works

George Berkeley

1685–1753

George Berkeley was an Irish philosopher, a fellow of Trinity College Dublin, and later Bishop of Cloyne. His immaterialism challenged the idea of matter existing independently of minds and explained reality through minds and their ideas. His investigations of perception and knowledge also extended to vision, natural science, religion, and questions of social life.

Perception · Minds and ideas · Immaterialism

Selected works

  • An Essay Towards a New Theory of Vision (1709)
  • A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge (1710)
  • Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous (1713)

Related research and publications

  • Modern Philosophy, Issue 1: Editing and Reception of Berkeley’s Works (Chinese)
  • Berkeley’s World: An Examination of the Three Dialogues
  • George Berkeley and the world

David Hume

1711–1776

David Hume was a Scottish philosopher, historian, and essayist. His study of human nature explored understanding, emotion, and morality, examining the role of habit in causal inference and the limits of reason. He investigated how sentiment and sympathy inform moral judgment, and extended his work to religion, political economy, and the history of England.

Causation and habit · Sentiment and morality · Scepticism and human nature

Selected works

  • A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–1740)
  • An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (1748)
  • An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals (1751)
Profile and works: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy

About

A meeting place for historical inquiry and contemporary questions

The Center of Modern Thought at Yangzhou University brings together historical depth, an international outlook and a concern for public life. It connects the study of modern intellectual traditions with philosophy, culture, technology and society, supporting research, interdisciplinary collaboration and open academic exchange.

Our work places early modern European thought in conversation with contemporary questions about technology, artificial intelligence, modernity and values, while supporting dialogue between Chinese and Western intellectual traditions.

Research

Four areas of inquiry

Early modern European thought

Descartes, Leibniz, Berkeley and their textual traditions; reason, subjectivity, nature, experience and method.

Modernity and contemporary questions

Technology, artificial intelligence, globalisation, ecological challenges and conflicts of values, approached through intellectual history.

Chinese–Western intellectual dialogue

Conceptual history, comparative inquiry, translation and the reinterpretation of philosophical traditions.

Intellectual history in public life

Books, public lectures and digital resources that connect research with education and public culture.
